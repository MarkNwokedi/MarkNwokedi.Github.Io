# Book Note System — A Starter Guide

*Your practical, day-to-day guide to reading non-fiction, retaining it, and being able
to explain and write about it. Tailored to: physical books → Supernote Manta (Book
Capture template) → spaced-repetition retention loop.*

Keep this open for your first ~5 books. After that it'll be muscle memory.

---

## The one idea behind all of it

**Reading is passive recognition; retention requires active production.** Every step
below exists to make you *produce* something — a recall, an explanation, a note — instead
of just re-reading. If a step ever feels a little effortful, that's the point (it's
"desirable difficulty"). Highlighting and re-reading feel productive but are weak; they
create an *illusion* of knowing. The work is in the recall and the notes.

---

## Your setup at a glance

| Layer | Tool | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Capture + thinking** | Supernote Manta + **Book Capture** template | Read, mark, recall, distill — by hand |
| **Permanent notes** | Slip-box notebook (or Obsidian) | One idea per note, linked, kept forever |
| **Retention + reminders** | Anki / RemNote (spaced repetition) | Tells you what to review, when |
| **Synthesis (optional)** | NotebookLM | Quiz yourself / compare across books |

Handwriting is an advantage here — it's slower and more effortful than typing, which means
better encoding. Use it.

## The workflow (four phases)

### 1. Before a book — *5 minutes, once per book*
- **Inspect it first.** Skim the cover, table of contents, intro, and index. Get the shape
  of the argument before committing.
- **Decide why you're reading it.** Write 1–2 questions you want the book to answer. These
  go in the **Guiding question(s)** box and aim your attention.

### 2. While reading — *one Book Capture page per chapter*
- **Pencil in hand.** Underline/bracket sparingly — only what matters. Capturing everything
  = capturing nothing.
- In the margin or the **Notes** column, jot **why** a passage matters or how it connects —
  not just what it says. (Asking "why is this true?" is itself a retention technique.)
- Use the **Cues / Keywords** column for 2–4 keywords per page — these become your search
  tags later (the Manta searches handwriting).
- Don't transcribe yet. Notes here are *meant* to be messy and follow the chapter.

### 3. After each chapter — **the most important step** *(~10 min)*
Do these **in order**:
1. **Close the book.** In **Free recall**, write — from memory — what the chapter argued
   and the takeaway. This single habit does most of the work. It'll feel harder than
   re-reading. Good.
2. **Check** your recall against your marks; fill gaps.
3. **Distill 2–4 Atomic note seeds** — the *ideas* worth keeping, each as one short claim
   in your own words (see the Atomic Notes section below).
4. Log any **Gaps**: what you didn't understand, and — once resolved — *how you closed it*
   (re-read, looked it up, another book). This is an honesty check that you actually got it.

### 4. Weekly — *~20–30 min*
- **Convert** the week's Supernote pages to text (handwriting recognition → export) and pull
  the atomic seeds into **permanent notes** (one idea each, fully written, linked).
- **Make/approve flashcards** from those permanent notes (or let an AI draft them).
- **Do your spaced-repetition reviews** — a few minutes most days (see Retention Loop).
- Once a month, or when an idea-cluster forms, **write something** — a short post/essay from
  your notes. Teaching it is the deepest retention step *and* your end goal.

## How to fill each zone of the Book Capture page

| Zone | What goes here | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Book / Ch. / Date** | Identity, so the page is findable later | Start of chapter |
| **Guiding question(s)** | 1–2 questions you want answered | Before reading |
| **Cues / Keywords** | 2–4 search tags per page | While reading |
| **Notes (while reading)** | Raw, multi-idea, follows the chapter — *not* atomic | While reading |
| **Free recall** | From memory, book closed: argument + takeaway | After the chapter |
| **Atomic note seeds** | 2–4 single ideas, your words (checkbox each) | After the chapter |
| **Gaps** | *what it was* / *how it was closed* | As they come up / once resolved |

> The **Notes** zone is supposed to be messy and cover many ideas. The **Atomic seeds**
> zone is where you decompose those into separate, single ideas. Don't confuse the two.

---

## Atomic notes — the core skill

A chapter contains **many** ideas. An atomic note holds **exactly one**. So one chapter
becomes **several** atomic notes — you're *decomposing* the chapter, not summarizing it.

**A note is atomic when:**
- You can **title it with the idea itself** — "Retrieval beats rereading" — not with a
  topic ("Memory") or a location ("Chapter 3").
- It's in **your own words**, a full sentence, standing on its own.
- You could **link it to a note from a totally different book** and it would still make
  sense.

**Gut check:** if describing the note needs the word *"and,"* it's probably **two** notes.

**Why bother:** one idea = one *connectable, reusable* unit. That's what lets you link ideas
across books, what forces real understanding, and what turns your notes into draft material
for writing.

*Example — one chapter on "anchoring" might yield:*
- "Anchoring: an arbitrary number drags your estimate toward it."
- "Anchoring works even when the anchor is obviously random."
- "In negotiation, the first offer sets the anchor for the range."

Be selective. Not every fact becomes a note — only ideas worth keeping. Three sharp atomic
notes beat a full-page summary.

## The retention loop (this is what makes it stick + reminds you)

Notes alone don't create memory — **spaced retrieval** does. Wire your seeds into a
spaced-repetition app so it schedules reviews and *reminds* you:

1. Turn key permanent notes into **flashcards** — ask a question, answer from memory.
   Phrase the front as a real question ("Why does retrieval beat rereading?"), not a
   keyword.
2. Use **Anki** or **RemNote** (FSRS algorithm). It shows you each card right before you'd
   forget it, and fewer cards over time.
3. **Review a few minutes most days.** Turn on the app's daily notification — that's your
   reminder to stay on top of it.
4. **Spacing scales with your goal:** to remember something for a year, reviews stretch to
   weeks/months apart. The app handles this; you just show up.

**Pairing:** retrieval + spacing together are far stronger than either alone — which is
exactly what spaced-repetition software automates.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Collector's fallacy.** Saving/highlighting ≠ knowing. An untouched archive grows your
  library, not your knowledge. The note in your own words is the asset.
- **Passive re-reading & highlighting.** Feels productive, builds little. Replace with free
  recall.
- **Over-tooling.** Don't perfect the system instead of using it. Two notebooks and a
  keyword habit is enough. The point is to **read, recall, and write** — not to garden
  tools.
- **Summarizing instead of decomposing.** A chapter summary is one blob; you want several
  atomic, linkable ideas.
- **Skipping the recall step** because it's harder than re-reading. That difficulty *is* the
  learning.

## One-page cheat sheet

```
BEFORE   → inspect the book; write 1–2 guiding questions.
DURING   → light marks; note WHY it matters; keywords in the cue column.
AFTER    → 1. close book, FREE RECALL from memory
            2. check against marks
            3. distill 2–4 ATOMIC SEEDS (one idea each, your words)
            4. log GAPS (what it was / how closed)
WEEKLY   → export → permanent notes → flashcards → spaced reviews → (monthly) write.

ATOMIC NOTE = one idea, titled by the idea, your words, linkable.
             If it needs "and", it's two notes.

THE RULE OF THUMB: produce, don't re-read. If it's a little hard, it's working.
```
